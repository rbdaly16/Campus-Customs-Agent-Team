Three things worked across all three tickets:

  • The hand-offs carried facts, not just tasks. The Boss never re-did a specialist's work. It passed confirmed numbers forward: Ollie's "8 on hand, 12 short" went to Shelly, and Shelly's "$58.00 × 8 = $464.00" went to Delphine.
  • Every run ended with a clear list of what a person still had to do. For example, "Approve payment request #1 for $840 to Bulldog Print Co.", "Confirm the vendor release and schedule for the size S reprint", and "Place a restock order with Bulldog Print Co for 12 × CC-HOOD-NAVY size M if the shop intends to fulfill all 20." These lists are what keep 101 and 103 honestly at "Awaiting human input".
  • The crew gave estimates without overpromising. Ollie turned the vendor's 5-day lead time into "September 5, 2026 is only an earliest estimate if released today, not a confirmed arrival". Delphine called it "the earliest estimated arrival" in the club's draft, and promised Tauhid no date at all while the reprint is unconfirmed.

3 · What would have been simpler as one agent with tools

Honestly, all three of these tickets would have been simpler and cheaper for one "shop clerk" agent with the same MCP tools, and #102 most of all. Rent is a straight line:

  • get_ticket
  • get_rent_due_and_cash_balance
  • list_payments
  • list_payment_requests
  • request_payment_approval
  • update_ticket

That's about six tool calls and roughly seven model turns, my estimate, against the 16 requests the team actually used. The team's overhead shows up in the audit trail:

  • The same lookups, repeated. get_ticket was called 3 times on #101 and 3 times on #103, and on #102 Hermie and Shelly each read lease 1's rent separately, because every specialist starts from scratch.
  • Instructions retyped at every hand-off. Each hand-off is the Boss rewriting facts into a new task (194–661 characters each), and each specialist re-reads its whole prompt. That's why three simple tickets cost 140,444 input tokens.
  • A game of telephone. In the rehearsal run, Shelly didn't trust the stock facts she was handed on #103 and asked Ollie again. One agent can't lose track of what it already knows.

What the team buys that one agent wouldn't:

  • Walls that keep secrets in. Delphine's tool list has no cost or cash tools, so she can't put "$22 unit cost" or "$160 left in checking" in a customer email. A single agent with every tool could.
  • One door for money. Only Shelly can call request_payment_approval, so a confused customer-facing step can't file a payment.
  • Focused rules. The discount and payment policies live in one short prompt instead of a giant one.

Takeaway: today's volume (3 tickets, one shop) doesn't justify five agents on its own; the permission boundaries are the real reason for the team. A good middle ground: one Boss that does the read-only lookups itself, and hands off only the two risky jobs, money (Accounting) and words a customer will read (Customer Service).

C. A manager's view: "show me everything the crew did with my money"

The data for a manager's console already exists:

  • every agent step, MCP call, and hand-off in audit_trail.json;
  • who prepared and who approved each payment, in payment_requests (requested_by = "accounting agent", decided_by);
  • signed notes on every ticket.

The crew can write the manager's daily briefing from those same tools (list_tickets, list_payment_requests, list_payments, get_cash_balance, list_leases). For this run it would read: "3 tickets; $3,240 out across 2 approved payments; checking $160; 2 drafts still to send." The sign-in itself isn't an agent job; it's an app feature (a login provider plus role checks on the approve, close, and reset routes). Our run shows why it's needed: payments #1 and #2 were approved by the typed-in name "Claude (for Robert Daly)", and your later test approvals show "R. Daly". The database can't tell whether either name is real. With a manager login, only managers would see the Cash tab and bottles, and staff would only send replies.

5 · Three new problems this team could not solve, and what it would need

A. Learning what humans approve: an approval score on every draft

The problem: every reply needs a human review, and the reviews catch the same things. In this run, 101 and 103 are both sitting at "Awaiting human input" for exactly that review. Both drafts used internal SKU codes ("CC-TEE-WHITE", "CC-HOOD-NAVY"). The 101 draft named a shelf location ("Aisle B"), and the 103 draft quoted policy language ("discount eligibility requires at least 10 units on the order"). A human would probably edit all of these, every time.

Why the team can't do it today: the app never records what the human did with a draft. Drafts live only in an agent's final output, and "Human steps done ✓" records that the steps happened, not whether the reply was sent as written, edited, or thrown out. There is nothing to learn from.

What it would need:

  • New tables: drafts (ticket, author agent, text, version) and draft_reviews (sent as-is / edited / rejected, the edited text, the reason, the reviewer).
  • New MCP tools: save_draft, record_draft_review, list_draft_reviews, and score_draft.
  • A sixth crew member, a reviewer (Pearl the oyster, who grows pearls by learning from grit). Pearl scores each draft before it reaches you, e.g. "62% likely approved as-is; likely edits: say 'white Bulldog tee' instead of 'CC-TEE-WHITE', and drop 'Aisle B'". She learns at first from your past approvals and rejections used as examples. Later, she's checked weekly: do the drafts she scored 90% actually get approved about 90% of the time?
  • The payoff: she hands Delphine the likely edits before drafting, so drafts start out closer to what you'd send.
  • Guardrails: a high score earns a faster one-click review, never an automatic send. Anything promising a price, a discount, a date, or money stays a full human review. The goal is fewer edits per review, not zero reviews.

B. Buying stock: placing and tracking restock orders with vendors

The problem: #103 ended with "place a restock order for 12 × CC-HOOD-NAVY size M", and #101 depended on a reprint nobody could see. The crew can recommend a purchase but can't make one, and it can't explain the $840: invoice 501 doesn't say how many shirts it covers, so the cost per shirt is unknown.

What it would need:

  • New tables: a purchase_orders table (vendor, SKU, size, qty, quoted price, status, expected date) and a vendor price list, since pricing.unit_cost isn't a vendor's quote and says nothing about rush fees.
  • New MCP tools: get_vendor_quote; create_purchase_order (a draft that washes up as a bottle, approved by a person like payments); mark_po_received (adds the units to inventory, so Ollie's counts update); and a vendor-email tool that only sends after approval.
  • A new Purchasing agent (Pelly the pelican, who hauls deliveries). Pelly works with Shelly so no order goes out that the chest can't cover. Given the $160 balance, that rule would have mattered right away.

C. Money coming in: sales, deposits, and a real cash forecast

The problem: the Cash tab only ever goes down: −$840.00, −$2,400.00, and $0.00 in. The app can't record Tauhid buying an M tee for $28 or the club paying $464 for 8 hoodies. Stock never decreases when something sells. And because the lease's due date doesn't move forward after payment #2, a forecast would still think September's rent is due. The shop finished this run with $160 and nothing in the system that could predict next month's $2,400 rent.

What it would need:

  • New tables: orders and sales.
  • New MCP tools:
    • record_sale: lowers inventory.qty and raises cash;
    • hold_stock: sets aside the 8 hoodies while the club decides;
    • take_deposit: e.g. 50% upfront on bulk club orders;
    • advance_lease_period: runs when rent is paid, so the due date moves to the next month;
    • cash_forecast: rent due, open invoices, and expected sales over the next 30 days.
  • A new Cashier agent to take payments, through a payment processor's test mode first, with refunds always going to a person.
  • A forecasting duty for Shelly, so a warning like "you'll be $2,240 short on rent unless $2,240 of sales come in" appears before you approve the next bottle, not after.

Bottom line

The crew got the hard, high-stakes parts right:

  • It verified the landlord before paying.
  • It never moved a dollar without a person's approval.
  • It never showed a customer a cost or a cash balance.
  • It landed on the exact cash outcome we planned: $3,400.00 → $160.00.

Its gaps are about business judgment (the $20-margin-vs-$840-invoice trade-off on #101, the missing bulk offer on #103) and about how the app records human work. In the rehearsal, tickets 101 and 103 were closed as "resolved" before anyone sent the replies. That gap is now closed: in this run they correctly end at awaiting_human_input, and only 102 is resolved. The next three investments would be:

  • a draft-approval reviewer, so human review shrinks over time;
  • purchasing tools, so restocks can actually be ordered;
  • sales and forecasting, so the treasure chest can go up as well as down.
